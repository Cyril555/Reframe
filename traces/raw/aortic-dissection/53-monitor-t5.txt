## Good catch. This is now a probable Type A dissection until CT says otherwise

A 36 mmHg arm differential plus a widened mediastinum, with the history and inferior ST elevation, makes acute aortic dissection (likely Type A with RCA involvement) the working diagnosis. Holding anticoagulation and getting CT is the right call.

### Immediate management while waiting for CT

**1. Antithrombotics**
- Give no aspirin, ticagrelor, heparin or thrombolytic. Tell the cath lab team to stand down. Don't activate for primary PCI.
- If any antiplatelet or anticoagulant has already been given, tell cardiothoracic surgery and the anaesthetist now.

**2. Location and monitoring**
- Resus bay with continuous ECG and SpO2.
- Arterial line if it can be placed without delaying CT. Use the **right arm**, because the left may read falsely low if the subclavian is involved.
- Two large-bore IVs.
- Catheter for urine output if time allows.

**3. Bloods**
- Group and **crossmatch** (suggest 4-6 units, per local massive transfusion practice), FBC, U&E, coagulation and fibrinogen, troponin, lactate, and a gas.
- Don't wait for results before CT.

**4. Pain control**
- Titrated IV opioid (fentanyl or morphine). Pain drives the catecholamine surge and the shear stress.

**5. Anti-impulse therapy (heart rate first, then BP)**
- **Targets:** HR about 60 and systolic BP 100-120 mmHg, or the lowest level that keeps organ perfusion adequate.
- **First line: IV beta-blocker.**
  - Esmolol: about 500 mcg/kg bolus, then 50-300 mcg/kg/min. It is short-acting and titratable, which suits a patient who may deteriorate.
  - Alternatively labetalol: 20 mg IV over 2 minutes, then 20-80 mg every 10 minutes, or an infusion of 0.5-2 mg/min.
- **Second line, after the heart rate is controlled:** if systolic BP is still above 120, add a vasodilator such as GTN or sodium nitroprusside. Never start a vasodilator before rate control, because reflex tachycardia increases aortic wall stress.
- **Contraindications and cautions for beta-blockade:**
  - Acute severe aortic regurgitation, where it blocks the compensatory tachycardia.
  - Tamponade or hypotension.
  - Bradycardia or heart block. Inferior MI can cause these, so check the rhythm.
  - If beta-blockers are contraindicated, a non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker (diltiazem or verapamil) is an alternative for rate control.
- **Do not lower pressure aggressively if he is hypotensive or shocked.** See below.

**6. Check for complications now**
- **Bedside echo (do not delay CT):** look for pericardial effusion or tamponade, aortic root dilation, an intimal flap, and aortic regurgitation.
- **Neuro and limb exam:** stroke signs, paraplegia, limb pulses, and abdominal tenderness for mesenteric ischaemia.
- **Repeat ECG** to follow the inferior changes.

**7. If he becomes hypotensive**
- Think tamponade, acute severe AR, rupture, or RCA malperfusion.
- **Do not give beta-blockers or vasodilators.**
- Get urgent cardiothoracic surgery input. This may mean going to theatre or hybrid theatre on a bedside echo diagnosis if he is too unstable for CT.
- Give blood products for rupture or bleeding. Use cautious volume only for true hypovolaemia. Aim for permissive hypotension, not full normalisation of BP.
- For tamponade, avoid routine pericardiocentesis. If he is peri-arrest, controlled or limited drainage may be considered as a bridge, but discuss with surgery first.

**8. Escalation and logistics**
- **Call cardiothoracic surgery and the aortic team now.** Don't wait for the CT report. Give them the arm differential, the CXR, the ECG and the antithrombotic status.
- Alert the anaesthetist and theatre.
- Request **CT angiography of the whole aorta** (ECG-gated chest, plus abdomen and pelvis, if available), and ask for an immediate verbal read.
- Send him to CT with a monitored escort and resus equipment. Keep the beta-blocker infusion running.
- If CT confirms Type A, the plan is emergency surgery. If CT shows Type B, the plan is usually medical management and BP control, with the vascular team deciding about intervention if there is malperfusion or rupture.

- **Your Hb and fibrinogen** matter for theatre planning, so get the early bloods sent.
- The doses above are typical. Check them against your local protocol, his weight, and his renal and hepatic function.
