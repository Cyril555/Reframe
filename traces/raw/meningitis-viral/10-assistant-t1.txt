## Bottom line
Fever, headache, neck stiffness and photophobia in a 19-year-old university student is **bacterial meningitis (including meningococcal disease) until proven otherwise**. The absence of a rash does not lower the risk. A significant minority of meningococcal cases never develop a non-blanching rash, and it can appear late.

## Why this patient is higher risk
- Age 15-24 and university life (halls, close contact, freshers' events) carry increased meningococcal carriage and risk.
- Please ask about MenACWY and MenB vaccination status. Vaccination reduces the risk but does not exclude disease.

## Differential
- **Bacterial meningitis:** meningococcus, pneumococcus. Must not be missed.
- **Meningococcal sepsis** with or without meningitis.
- **Viral meningitis:** the most common cause overall, but it is a diagnosis of exclusion.
- **Encephalitis**, especially HSV. Look for confusion, behaviour change, seizures or focal signs.
- **Subarachnoid haemorrhage:** less likely given the fever and 2-day course, but consider it if the onset was sudden or thunderclap.
- **Other causes:** severe influenza or other viral illness, EBV, sinusitis or mastoiditis with intracranial extension, brain abscess, and (if relevant) malaria after travel.

## Immediate actions
1. **ABCDE and sepsis screen** now. I only have the temperature so far, so please record HR, BP, RR, SpO2, capillary refill, GCS and capillary glucose.
2. **Examine fully for:**
   - Skin: look at the whole body, including the soles, palms, conjunctivae and mucosa. Do a glass test on any spots.
   - Meningism: neck stiffness, Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs.
   - Neurology: focal deficits, papilloedema, new confusion or drowsiness, seizures.
   - Cold peripheries, limb pain or mottling, which are early sepsis signs.
3. **IV access and bloods:**
   - Blood cultures (before antibiotics, but do not delay them)
   - Meningococcal and pneumococcal PCR
   - FBC, CRP, U&E, LFT, glucose, lactate, coagulation screen, gas
4. **Antibiotics without delay.** Do not wait for LP or imaging.
   - Give empirical IV antibiotics, ideally within 1 hour of recognition. If cultures cannot be taken quickly, treat first.
   - In the UK this is typically ceftriaxone 2 g IV. Please follow your local guideline, and check allergy history.
5. **Dexamethasone:** consider it, per local protocol, for suspected bacterial meningitis. It is usually given just before or with the first antibiotic dose.
6. **Aciclovir:** add IV aciclovir if there is any suggestion of encephalitis, such as altered consciousness, seizures or focal signs.
7. **Fluids and escalation:** give fluids if there is evidence of sepsis. Involve senior and critical care early if there is shock, a falling GCS or purpura.

## Lumbar puncture and CT
- Do LP when it is safe, but only after antibiotics are given if there is any delay.
- CT head **before** LP only if there are contraindications to LP, such as:
  - GCS ≤ 12 or a falling GCS
  - Focal neurological signs
  - Papilloedema
  - Seizures
  - Significant immunocompromise
  - Signs of raised ICP
- Also avoid LP if there is shock, a coagulopathy or local infection at the puncture site.
- CSF tests: cell count and differential, protein, glucose with a paired plasma glucose, Gram stain and culture, bacterial and viral PCR (including HSV and VZV).

## Infection control and public health
- Use droplet precautions until 24 hours of effective antibiotics, if meningococcal disease is suspected.
- Notify your local health protection or public health team promptly on clinical suspicion. Do not wait for confirmation.
- Contact prophylaxis applies to close contacts, which for a student means housemates and partners. The public health team will usually advise and coordinate this.

## Disposition
- Do not discharge. This patient needs admission, and in practice a resus or high-dependency level of monitoring while they are being assessed.
- Reassess frequently with observations and GCS. Escalate if there is any deterioration.

## Information that would change the plan
- Full vital signs and GCS
- Vaccination history, recent travel and sick contacts
- Allergies, immunosuppression, and any history of head injury or ENT infection
- Any new confusion, seizure or focal deficit

This is decision support. Please check dosing and pathways against your local guideline and with your senior or microbiology team.
